Getting Your First 100 Users on Campus Without a Marketing Budget

Published September 2026 · Category: Growth · By Blazonshots Editorial Team · Editorial Policy

Student founders often assume the reason their app has no users yet is that not enough people know it exists, and the natural response is to want a bigger audience — a viral post, a paid ad, a feature in the student newspaper. In practice, the first hundred users on a campus rarely come from broad awareness. They come from a small number of specific, trusted spaces, reached deliberately, one at a time.

Start smaller than feels comfortable

The instinct to launch to the whole university at once is understandable but usually counterproductive at this stage. A product that is not yet fully working, with rough edges the team already knows about, does much better being tested by fifty people who are forgiving and give direct feedback than by five thousand people forming a first impression that is hard to change once formed. Choose one specific, contained group first — a single course cohort, a single hall of residence, a single club — where you already have some legitimate reason to be present, and treat that group as the entire initial audience.

Borrow trust instead of building it from zero

New products have no reputation of their own, which makes cold outreach to strangers slow and unreliable. The more effective path is finding a person or group that already has the community's trust and asking them to introduce the product, rather than introducing it yourself. This might be a course representative, a society committee, a residence hall group chat admin, or a lecturer willing to mention a relevant tool once in class. A recommendation from someone the community already trusts carries far more weight in the first days than any amount of polished marketing copy from an unknown source.

This only works if the request is specific and low-effort for the person you are asking. "Please tell everyone about my app" is easy to ignore. "Would you be willing to share this one message in the group chat this week?" — with the message already written for them — is easy to say yes to.

Be physically present, not just digitally announced

Digital-only launches are easy to scroll past. A founder who shows up in person — at a table outside the library during a busy hour, at a stall during a fresher's fair, in the queue for a popular lecture — has a structural advantage that no online post can replicate: it is much harder to ignore a person standing in front of you than a notification. The conversion rate from a genuine two-minute in-person conversation, where you can answer a specific objection on the spot, is almost always higher than the conversion rate from a post someone glances at for two seconds while scrolling.

This does not need to be elaborate. A handwritten sign, a QR code, and a clear one-sentence explanation of the problem the product solves is enough. What matters is being present, repeatedly, in the specific places your first-sentence description of your target user already spends time.

Make the first users feel like collaborators, not just numbers

The people who sign up in the first two weeks are disproportionately important, not because of their number but because of what they can become: vocal advocates who bring the next fifty people, or silent churns who quietly stop opening the app and say nothing. The difference usually comes down to whether they feel heard. Personally messaging early users to ask what is and is not working, actually shipping a visible fix based on their specific feedback within days, and telling them directly "you asked for this, here it is" turns a first user into someone invested in the product's success rather than a one-time downloader.

Let the second hundred come from the first hundred

Once a first, contained community is genuinely using and enjoying the product, the most reliable source of the next group of users is the first group itself — not through a formal referral programme, but through the ordinary way that useful things get mentioned between friends, coursemates, and people in the same residence hall. This spread only happens if the product is actually good enough to be worth mentioning; no amount of tactical growth advice compensates for a product that does not yet deliver on its core promise. Growth tactics accelerate word of mouth that already exists. They rarely manufacture it from nothing.

Expanding to a second and third group should follow the same pattern as the first — small, specific, reached through a trusted introduction rather than broad announcement — rather than jumping straight to a university-wide push. Each successful small community becomes both a source of proof ("this is already used by two hundred students in halls") and a source of the next trusted introduction, which compounds far more reliably than any single large marketing push would.
