How Students Can Protect Their Personal Data on Campus Apps

Published October 2026 · Category: Digital Trust & Safety · By Blazonshots Editorial Team · Editorial Policy

Most students hand over personal information to campus apps without much thought, because each individual request — a name, a timetable, a location permission — seems small on its own. The risk is rarely any single piece of data. It is the accumulation of many small, low-friction requests across a dozen different apps, most of which you will stop actively thinking about within a week of installing them.

Before installing, check who actually built it

A genuinely useful first habit is looking at who publishes an app before handing over any information — an official university department, a registered company with a real About page and contact details, or an anonymous developer account with no other context. This does not mean avoiding every app built by an independent student developer; many of the most useful campus tools start exactly that way. It means spending thirty seconds checking, because that thirty seconds is the cheapest possible protection against an app that turns out to be careless, or worse, with the data it collects.

Permissions should match what the app actually needs to do

A timetable app asking for constant background location access, or a study tool asking for access to your contacts, is a mismatch worth noticing. The general rule that works well in practice: a permission request should map obviously onto a feature you can see in the app. If it does not — if you cannot think of a reason the app needs what it is asking for — that is a reasonable moment to decline the permission or look for a different app entirely. Most phones let you grant a permission only while the app is in use rather than permanently, which is a reasonable middle ground for apps that have a legitimate but occasional need for something like location.

Think about what a screenshot or post could reveal beyond its intended audience

Campus social apps and group chats often feel private because the audience is small and familiar, which leads to sharing more openly than the same person would on a fully public platform. It is worth remembering that screenshots travel far beyond their original context, and visibility settings do not always work the way the interface implies — a pattern discussed in more depth in our piece on data privacy mistakes campus app builders make. A reasonable working assumption for anything posted in a group space, even a seemingly private one, is that it could eventually be seen outside that group, and to post accordingly.

Clean up accounts you no longer use

Every semester tends to add a few new campus apps — one for a specific class, one for an event, one a friend recommended that never quite stuck. Each of these accounts remains a small, quiet source of stored personal data long after the app itself is forgotten. Periodically reviewing installed apps and deleting accounts for ones no longer in active use, rather than simply leaving them dormant on a phone, meaningfully reduces the number of places your information could be exposed if any one of those services is ever compromised.

Lock down the accounts that everything else depends on

Your email account and your phone number are the keys to almost every other account you own, because password resets go to them. Protecting those two first gives you the biggest return for the least effort:

  • Turn on two-step verification for your main email and for WhatsApp. WhatsApp's two-step verification adds a PIN that is needed to register your number on a new phone, which blocks the common trick of persuading someone to share a six-digit code.
  • Never share a verification code. No genuine bank, school portal, app or friend needs the code sent to your phone. A message asking for it — even from a familiar contact — should be treated as an account takeover attempt, because that contact's account may already be compromised.
  • Use a different password for your email than for any campus app. Small apps are more likely to be breached, and reused passwords let attackers move from a forgotten app to your main inbox.
  • Use a password manager or a written list kept somewhere safe rather than one memorable password everywhere.

Shared phones, cyber cafés and lab computers

Many students sign in to school portals, email and cloud storage from computers they do not own: departmental labs, business centres, a roommate's laptop. Each of these can keep you signed in long after you leave. When using a shared device, use a private or incognito browser window, do not tick "remember me", sign out of every account before you go, and never save passwords in a shared browser. If you realise you left an account signed in, most email and social services let you sign out of all other sessions from your account's security settings.

Phones get borrowed too. A screen lock, a separate lock on WhatsApp or banking apps where your phone supports it, and notification previews hidden on the lock screen all stop casual snooping without making the phone harder to use.

Your rights over your data in Nigeria

Students are not only responsible for protecting their data; organisations that collect it have obligations too. The Nigeria Data Protection Commission oversees the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, which requires organisations processing personal data to have a lawful reason, keep it secure and respect individuals' rights, such as asking what data is held about you and requesting correction or deletion in appropriate cases. Practically, this means you can email the publisher of a campus app and ask what data they hold about you and how to delete your account. A responsive answer is a good sign. No answer, or no contact address at all, tells you how seriously that app takes your information.

Being careful is not the same as avoiding useful tools

None of this is an argument for avoiding campus apps altogether — many of them genuinely make student life easier, and the goal is not suspicion of every tool. It is a small set of habits, repeated consistently: check who built it, question permissions that do not match the feature, assume shared content can travel further than intended, and clean up what you no longer use. Together, these habits cost very little time and meaningfully reduce how much of your personal information is sitting, unmonitored, across apps you may not even remember installing.

Spotting phishing in school portals and group chats

Many data leaks begin with a convincing message rather than a hacked app. Common versions aimed at students include fake course registration or school fee links, messages claiming your portal account will be suspended, fake scholarship or internship offers asking for a processing fee, and messages from a friend's hijacked WhatsApp account asking for a code or urgent money. A few checks catch most of them. Look closely at the web address before entering a password: official university portals use the institution's own domain, not a free website builder or a shortened link. Be wary of urgency, spelling mistakes and requests to pay into a personal account. When a friend asks for money or a code by message, call them first. If you entered your password on a page you now doubt, change it immediately from the official site and check your account's recent activity. Frequently asked questions

What personal data should students never share on campus apps?

Verification codes, passwords, your BVN or NIN, bank card details and your exact home address should never be entered into an unfamiliar campus app or sent in a chat. Legitimate student apps rarely need any of them.

How do I know if an app is collecting too much data?

Compare its permission requests with its visible features. A study planner that wants your contacts or constant location is collecting more than it needs, and you can deny those permissions in your phone's settings.

How do I delete my data from an app I no longer use?

Look for a delete account option in the app's settings first, since uninstalling the app does not delete your account. If there is none, email the publisher and ask them to delete your account and data.

Is WhatsApp two-step verification worth turning on?

Yes. It takes a minute and blocks one of the most common ways student accounts are hijacked in Nigeria: tricking someone into sharing a registration code.
