To calculate your CGPA, multiply each course's credit units by the grade point you scored (A = 5, B = 4, C = 3, D = 2, E = 1 where your school uses it, F = 0), add up all those quality points across every semester so far, and divide by the total credit units you have registered. That single number, on a scale of 0 to 5, decides your class of degree. The rest of this guide shows the calculation step by step with real numbers, explains where universities differ, and shows how to work out what you need for the class you are aiming for.

The 5-point grading scale used in Nigerian universities

The National Universities Commission (NUC) briefly moved universities to a 4-point scale in 2017, then directed all of them to revert to the five-point scale from the 2018/2019 academic session. So almost every Nigerian university student today is graded out of 5. The table below shows the scale most universities use.

Using the wrong scale is the most common reason a student's own calculation does not match the result the department releases.

What "credit units" mean

Every course carries a number of credit units (also called credit load or units), usually between 1 and 6. The figure roughly reflects how many contact hours the course has per week. A 3-unit course counts three times as much towards your CGPA as a 1-unit course. You will find the units on your course registration form, your department's course outline or your portal's registration page. Keep a copy of the registration form every semester; it is the most reliable record you have.

How to calculate your GPA for one semester (step by step)

Your GPA (grade point average) covers one semester. Your CGPA (cumulative grade point average) covers every semester so far. Start with the GPA, because the CGPA is built from the same pieces.

  1. List every course you registered for that semester, with its credit units.
  2. Convert each score to a grade point using your university's scale.
  3. Multiply units by grade point for each course. The result is the course's quality points (some schools call them weighted grade points).
  4. Add up the quality points for all courses.
  5. Add up the credit units for all courses, including any you failed.
  6. Divide total quality points by total credit units.

How to calculate your CGPA across several semesters

The CGPA is not the average of your semester GPAs. It is total quality points from every semester divided by total credit units from every semester. The difference matters whenever your semesters carry different numbers of units.

Worked example 2: turning two semesters into a CGPA

Continue with the same student. In second semester they registered 18 units and earned 76 quality points (an A in a 4-unit course, an A in a 3-unit course, Bs in two 3-unit courses, a C in a 3-unit course and a B in a 2-unit course: 20 + 15 + 12 + 12 + 9 + 8 = 76). Their second-semester GPA is 76 ÷ 18 = 4.22.

The practical habit that follows: keep a running record of just two numbers, cumulative units and cumulative quality points. Each new semester, add that semester's units and points to the running totals and divide again. You never need to re-enter old results.

CGPA classes of degree: what each range means

Your final CGPA places you in a class of degree. Most universities use these bands:

1.50–2.39Third Class
1.00–1.49Pass (only where still awarded)

The Pass degree is the second place universities differ. NUC has said it abolished the Pass degree from its classification, with 1.50 as the minimum CGPA to graduate, but practice has not been uniform and some institutions' published regulations still show a 1.00–1.49 band. Many universities also use 1.50 as the line for "good academic standing": fall below it at the end of a session and you may be placed on probation, and staying there can lead to withdrawal. Your university's academic regulations are the final word, so read that section of your handbook once, early, rather than in final year.

What a carry-over does to your CGPA

A failed course is not just a missing grade. Its credit units still count in your total, but it contributes zero quality points, so it pulls your average down. When you retake and pass it (a carry-over), how the new result is counted depends on your university's rules. In many regulations both attempts count: the original F stays on record with its units, and the retake adds its units and points again.

Worked example 3: the cost of one failed 3-unit course

Take the student from example 2 (130 points over 34 units, CGPA 3.82). Suppose instead that one 3-unit B in second semester had been an F. They lose 12 points: 118 ÷ 34 = 3.47. One failed course has moved a 2:1 student into 2:2 territory for now.

Next session they retake the course and score a C (3 points × 3 units = 9). If both attempts count, the totals become 127 points over 37 units: 127 ÷ 37 = 3.43, before adding their other new courses. The pass helps, but the F never fully disappears. That is why clearing carry-overs at the first opportunity, with real preparation rather than a hurried resit, is worth so much. Universities also cap how many units you can register in a semester (24 is a common limit), so a pile of carry-overs can crowd out new courses and delay graduation.

How to work out the grades you need for your target class

The same arithmetic runs backwards. To find what you need in the remaining semesters:

  1. Estimate the total units for your whole programme (add up the course outline, or ask your level adviser).
  2. Multiply that total by your target CGPA. This is the number of quality points you need by graduation.
  3. Subtract the quality points you already have.
  4. Divide what is left by the units still to come. The answer is the average grade point you need from now on.

Say a student on a four-year programme expects 150 units in total and wants a 2:1 (3.50). They need 150 × 3.50 = 525 quality points. After two years they have 72 units and 238 points (CGPA 3.31). They need 525 − 238 = 287 more points from the remaining 78 units: 287 ÷ 78 = 3.68 average from here on. That means mostly Bs with some As, which is demanding but realistic. If the answer comes out above 5.00, the target is mathematically out of reach, and it is better to know that now and aim for the strongest result still possible.

Two further points help. First, high-unit courses move your CGPA the most, so put your best study hours into the 3- and 4-unit courses. Second, a final-year project often carries 4 to 6 units on its own, so treat it as several courses' worth of grade points.

Common mistakes when calculating CGPA

  • Averaging semester GPAs instead of dividing total points by total units.
  • Leaving failed courses out of the unit total. The units still count; only the points are zero.
  • Using the wrong scale, for example counting an E as 1 point when your department treats anything below 45 as F.
  • Counting courses that do not count. Some universities record certain GST or audited courses without adding them to the CGPA. Your handbook will say.
  • Rounding early. Keep full totals and only round the final answer to two decimal places.
  • Trusting a random calculator app. Many free CGPA apps are fine, but some ask for permissions or personal data they do not need. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between GPA and CGPA?

GPA is your average for one semester. CGPA is your average across every semester you have completed, calculated from total quality points and total credit units. Your class of degree is based on the final CGPA.

What CGPA is a first class in Nigeria?

On the 5-point scale, a first class is usually a CGPA of 4.50 to 5.00. A second class upper is 3.50 to 4.49 and a second class lower is 2.40 to 3.49. Some universities apply extra rules (for example no outstanding failed courses), so check your regulations.

Does a carry-over stop me from getting a first class?

Not automatically, but it makes it harder, because the failed attempt usually stays in your CGPA calculation. A few institutions also have rules about classification for students with failed courses, so ask your department.

Can I convert my 5-point CGPA to a 4.0 GPA?

There is no single official formula, and many foreign universities and scholarship bodies do their own evaluation from your transcript. If an application asks for a 4.0-scale figure, follow its instructions or ask the admissions office which conversion it accepts rather than guessing.

Once you have your number, the useful question is not "what is my CGPA?" but "what do I need from here?" Keep your running totals, check your handbook's scale once, and do the backwards calculation at the start of every semester so you know exactly where your effort will count most.
